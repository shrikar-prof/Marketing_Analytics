The chain has a depth problem on a short list of products

The panel gave away $797,186 in discounts, 15.7% of $5,063,618 list revenue, and the retailer paid $777,398 of it. The money sits in deep markdowns on a few hundred products, mostly in GROCERY and MEAT. No one can yet say which promotions pay back, because no promotion's extra sales were measured. Plan on $100K to $150K a year of recovered margin on the panel, but test it first.

Part A

The answer in plain language

Promotions leak $797K a year; most of it is deep markdowns the chain pays for itself

Answer

The question, as asked. How much list revenue leaks to discounts? Where does it concentrate? Which promotions fail to pay back? What margin comes back if the worst ones are trimmed? (PD)

The answer. The panel gave away $797,186, 15.7% of list revenue. The retailer paid $777,398 of it. The money sits in deep markdowns on a short list of products. Discount above a 25% depth ceiling is $227,564 a year, and 383 products hold half of it. Payback cannot be judged yet, because the data holds no measure of extra sales caused by a promotion. The planning case is $100K to $150K a year, resting on assumptions. Tested pilots should come before any chain-wide cut.

What should happen next

  1. Require sign-off for retailer-funded markdowns deeper than 25%; finance sign-off above 35%.
  2. Audit supplier funding received outside the till, then ask suppliers of the top 200 national-brand products to fund deep deals.
  3. Run a 12-week depth-cap test in 40 matched store pairs on the 383 products, excluding five traffic categories.
  4. Hold out 20% of stores from each mailer promotion to measure which events pay back.
  5. Bring silent markdowns into the approval calendar and log every price event at the till.

What is uncertain

  • No promotion's extra sales were measured; the recovery range assumes how shoppers respond.
  • Product cost and off-till supplier funding are missing; margin math uses an assumed 33% margin.
  • The model explains 17% of line-to-line variation; use it to rank, not to forecast.

B3. Four tree models tie; CatBoost was chosen and beats both baselines

Model

The model predicts each line's discount rate (0 to 1). It trains on weeks 1–39 (1,057,007 lines) and is tested once on later weeks (388,028 lines). Cross-validation used TimeSeriesSplit. (MC, HO s.3)

Tuning in plain words. Optuna tried 60 setting combinations per tree model with the TPE sampler. Parameter importance showed CatBoost cared most about random_strength (0.41) and iterations (0.27). It needed randomness against over-fitting and enough trees, more than depth. (TUN, HO s.5)

Why CatBoost. The tuned tree models sit within 0.001 of each other (HO s.5). LightGBM scored best (0.1511). CatBoost (0.1513) was within one standard deviation, and the notebook chose it as simpler (NB03 cell 30). The handoff adds that it handles categories without separate encoding (HO s.5).
